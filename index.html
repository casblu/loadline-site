Where it can help

A mine can switch off in seconds. If it reliably does so when supply is short, it behaves like a very large, fast demand-response resource. Duke's Nicholas Institute estimates the US grid could add ~76 GW of flexible load if it curtails only 0.25% of the time.

Where it can hurt

New large loads add to peak demand if they don't reliably curtail, and a mine that only responds to price may stay on when the grid is scarce. Studies also disagree on how much mining power is fossil-fuelled, and on local effects such as noise.

Why the model: ERCOT's proposed NPRR‑1337 (filed May 26, 2026) would disqualify loads that only switch off when prices are high. The checkbox illustrates that distinction. Shapes of the curves are invented.

Methodology & sources

Our rules

  • Every figure links to a source. If we can't cite it, we don't publish it.
  • Industry-reported and advocacy figures are labelled as such.
  • Where sources conflict, we show both, not an average.
  • Pins mark town-level locations reported publicly. They are not surveyed site coordinates.
  • Illustrative graphics say "illustrative" on them.
  • Gaps are stated as gaps: no official EIA data, no neutral polling found.

Known limits

  • Drafted Oct 15, 2026 from public documents; not independently audited.
  • Bitcoin Mining Council's own site couldn't be loaded when checked; its figure is via a secondary summary and is self-reported.
  • Miners are moving to AI hosting, so "mining" and "flexible compute" overlap.
  • No live grid data feed yet. For live conditions see ERCOT and Grid Status below.
